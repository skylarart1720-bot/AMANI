1. Vision & Problem Statement
Young people in Ghana and beyond who engage in protest, activism, digital expression, gender advocacy,
environmental justice, mental-health struggles or governance participation often face the same three gaps: they
don't know their rights, they don't know who to trust for information, and they don't know a safe, anonymous way to
ask for help. Misinformation, phishing and online harassment compound the risk. This system closes that gap with
a single, trustworthy front door — a website and an anonymous WhatsApp line — backed by an AI assistant that
informs, reassures and refers, but never replaces professional or emergency services.
The system deliberately follows Amnesty International's own working method: research the facts, protect the
individual, and mobilise the appropriate institutions — rather than tell people what action to personally take. It also
names and pushes back on harms Amnesty and partner bodies track closely in Ghana and the wider region,
including violent responses to protest, attacks on civic space, digital surveillance, and harmful traditional practices
such as witchcraft accusations against women and older persons.
2. Design Principles & Ethical Guardrails

Inform, don't instruct: the AI explains rights, laws and options, then refers to a real body or hotline — it never
tells a user to personally carry out a specific risky action (e.g. "go to the protest", "confront your abuser").

Anonymous by default: no phone-number-to-identity linking is stored beyond what WhatsApp itself requires
operationally; conversations are pseudonymous unless the user actively opts to share identifying details to be
connected to a named contact.

Do no harm / safe messaging: for suicide, self-harm and gender-based violence topics, the assistant follows
WHO/International Association for Suicide Prevention (IASP) safe-messaging principles — no method or means
detail, calm and hopeful tone, always paired with a live helpline or professional referral.

Human-in-the-loop escalation: any message indicating imminent danger (self-harm, threat of violence, arrest
in progress, trafficking) is flagged for priority human moderator handoff, never left to the bot alone.

Neutrality on contested politics: the system presents rights, laws and verified facts, not partisan opinions; it
never tells a user which political position to hold, only what their legal rights and safety options are.

Global & local law together: every answer that touches rights pairs the Ghanaian legal position with the
applicable international instrument (ICCPR, African Charter, UNCRC, CEDAW, etc.).

Radical transparency about limits: the bot always discloses that it is an AI, that it is not a lawyer, doctor or
counsellor, and that it can connect the user to one.

Privacy & consent first: data minimisation, encryption in transit and at rest, a visible "delete my chat history"
control, and no advertising or data resale — modelled on Amnesty's own Human Rights Friendly design
commitments and human-rights-based approach to technology.

Inclusive & intersectional: content and referral paths are reviewed for gender, disability, LGBTQ+, ethnic and
religious inclusiveness, including a dedicated, non-judgemental peer-support pathway for men and boys ("Men's
Circle") alongside the feminist/gender-justice pathway — not positioned as opposing tracks, but as two doors
into the same support ecosystem.

4. User Journey — How a Real Person Uses the System

Discovery: user finds the WhatsApp number or website via social media, campus posters, or an
Amnesty/partner-organisation referral.

First contact (anonymous): user texts the WhatsApp number or opens the web chat. No sign-up, no name
required. Bot greets them, states clearly it is an AI, explains confidentiality, and asks (via quick-reply buttons)
which of the 9 pillars is closest to their need — or lets them type freely.

Triage: a lightweight safety-classifier scans the message for urgent-risk signals (self-harm, imminent violence,
minors at risk, active detention). Urgent cases skip straight to a human-handoff queue with a locally relevant
emergency number surfaced immediately.

Information + referral: for non-urgent queries, the AI retrieves verified information (see Section 8) and gives a
short, plain-language answer, then offers 1–3 concrete next steps: a verified link, a hotline, an organisation's
contact, or "talk to a real person" handoff.

Localisation: if the user shares a region (optional, never demanded), the bot narrows referrals to that region's
police unit, hospital, CSO chapter, or legal-aid office.

Optional identification: only if the user chooses to be connected to a named case-worker (e.g. for legal aid
follow-up) does the system ask for contact details, under an explicit, separate consent step, stored encrypted
and deletable.

Exit & follow-up: user can end the chat anytime; a "forget this conversation" command permanently deletes
the anonymous thread. Optional satisfaction/feedback survey (2 taps) improves the service without collecting
identity.

7. AI / Chatbot Behaviour Specification
The assistant's system prompt and tool-access should encode the following behaviour rules as hard constraints,
independent of what a user asks it to do:

Always identify itself as an AI assistant, never impersonate a human counsellor or lawyer.

Answer only from the verified knowledge base and referral directory (RAG-grounded) — it does not freely
generate legal, medical or safety claims from unguided model memory.

For every substantive answer, name at least one concrete referral (organisation, hotline, or official source) the
user can contact next.

Refuse to give operational instructions for risky personal action (e.g. how to evade police, how to confront an
abuser, self-harm methods); instead it explains rights/options and refers to a body equipped to help.

On any signal of imminent risk to life (self-harm intent, active violence, a minor in danger), it immediately
surfaces the relevant crisis line and flags the conversation for human moderator review, without waiting for
further prompting.

Uses a warm, calm, non-judgemental tone; avoids alarmist or graphic language, consistent with WHO
safe-messaging guidance.

Declines to take or endorse a partisan political position; presents rights and facts, not opinions on contested
policy questions.

Supports short free-text queries and a structured menu (quick-reply buttons on WhatsApp) so users who don't
know what to type can still navigate the 9 pillars.

Logs a machine-readable "outcome tag" per conversation (e.g. information-given, referred-to-hotline,
escalated-to-human) for anonymised service reporting — never logs raw personal identifiers unless the user
explicitly opted into being contacted.
8. Verified-Information & Fact-Sourcing Pipeline
Rather than letting the AI browse the open internet live for every answer (which risks pulling in unverified or
manipulated content), the recommended design uses a curated-then-augmented pipeline:

Allow-listed source registry: a maintained list of trusted publishers — Amnesty International, UN OHCHR,
WHO, UNICEF, UNDP, Ghana's CHRAJ, Ghana Police Service, Ghana Health Service, Mental Health
Authority, Ghana Data Protection Commission, reputable Ghanaian outlets (GhanaWeb, Joy News,
MyJoyOnline, Graphic) and recognised legal-aid bodies.

Scheduled ingestion: a background job periodically fetches updates from these sources, extracts the relevant
facts, and stores them with source URL, publish date and a "last verified" timestamp — this becomes the RAG
knowledge base the chatbot draws from.

Human review gate: a small editorial/legal team (partner NGO staff or volunteer lawyers) reviews new or
changed entries before they go live, especially for legal-rights content, mirroring how Amnesty's own research is
vetted before publication.

Live web-search fallback (optional, rate-limited): for fast-moving news (e.g. a new law, a breaking protest
event), the system can call a live search API, but results are shown as "unverified — for your awareness" with a
link, clearly separated from the vetted knowledge-base answers, until reviewed and promoted into the
permanent knowledge base.

Freshness indicator: every answer footnotes "last verified on [date]" so users can judge currency.

9. Anonymous WhatsApp Helpline Design

Number & onboarding: a single published WhatsApp Business number; first message triggers an automated
welcome explaining what the service is, that it is anonymous, that it is not an emergency service, and offering a
menu of the 9 pillars plus "talk to a human" and "emergency numbers" quick replies.

Anonymity model: WhatsApp requires a phone number to deliver messages, so true anonymity from Meta/the
telecom is not possible — the system's commitment is that the platform itself does not store the user's name,
does not link the number to any other identity system, encrypts the number at rest, and purges numbers from
analytics after a short retention window (e.g. 30–90 days) unless the user has opted into ongoing case support.

Session & language: language auto-detected or chosen at onboarding (English, Twi, Ga, Ewe, Hausa);
session state kept only long enough to hold a conversation, not built into a persistent CRM profile.

Escalation path: a "talk to a human" quick reply hands off to a rotating roster of trained volunteers/partner-org
staff via a moderator dashboard, with the AI conversation history shown to the human agent only after user
consent (or automatically in a flagged imminent-risk case, disclosed to the user as standard practice).

Rate & abuse handling: automated throttling and basic spam/abuse detection so the line cannot be flooded or
used to harass staff; clearly stated acceptable-use notice.

Cost model: WhatsApp Business Platform (Cloud API) charges per conversation/message and is typically
accessed through a Meta-approved Business Solution Provider (BSP) such as Twilio, 360dialog or a regional
provider — budget for BSP platform fees plus Meta's per-message/category charges, which should be modelled
against expected monthly volume before committing to a provider.
10. Link & Phishing Safety Module
Supports pillar 5 (Digital Rights & Online Safety) by letting a user paste a suspicious link, phone number, or
screenshot text and get a safety verdict:

URLs are checked against Google Safe Browsing and VirusTotal reputation APIs, plus a locally maintained
block-list of known Ghanaian scam/phishing campaigns.

The bot returns a plain-language verdict ("this link shows signs of phishing — do not enter your details") plus a
short explanation of the red flags, never technical jargon.

For confirmed scams targeting a wide public (e.g. a fake mobile-money promotion), the system can flag the
case to the Ghana Cybersecurity Authority's incident-reporting channel with the user's consent.

The module never itself "fixes" a compromised account; it always refers the user to the relevant provider's
official recovery process or the Cybersecurity Authority.

12. Privacy, Security & End-to-End Encryption

Transport encryption: WhatsApp messages already travel over Meta's end-to-end encryption for
user-to-business chat where supported; the website chat widget uses TLS 1.3 plus application-layer encryption
of message payloads at rest.

Data minimisation: collect only what is operationally necessary (message content, chosen topic, optional
region); never require real names, national ID, or exact addresses.

Anonymisation & retention limits: conversation logs are pseudonymised (hashed session IDs, not phone
numbers) for analytics, with a short default retention period (e.g. 90 days) after which raw content is purged;
only aggregated, de-identified statistics are kept long-term.

Right to be forgotten: a user-triggered "delete my chat" command purges their thread immediately, in line with
the Ghana Data Protection Act, 2012 (Act 843) and GDPR-equivalent principles for any EU-based users or
funders.

Access control: role-based access for moderators/case-workers, full audit logging of any human access to a
conversation, and mandatory data-protection and safeguarding training for all staff/volunteers with access.

Consent-gated identification: a user is only ever identified if they explicitly choose to be connected to a
named case-worker for follow-up (e.g. legal aid); this is a distinct, clearly explained opt-in step, never a default.

Independent security review: a penetration test and a data-protection impact assessment (DPIA) before
public launch, given the sensitivity of the subject matter.

13. Brand & UI/UX Guidelines (Amnesty-Aligned)
Amnesty International's global identity uses Amnesty Yellow as its primary colour — used sparingly, as an "alert"
or call-to-action colour rather than a decorative fill — paired with black and white for contrast and legibility, plus a
restrained grey for supporting graphics. This system should borrow that logic rather than the literal logo (the
Amnesty candle mark itself is protected and should not be used unless in a formal, permitted partnership).

Neutral grey
#EDEDED /

Supporting UI chrome, secondary text, dividers.

Plain, calm, accessible language (target reading level: lower-secondary school) in every UI string, in line with
the tone described in Section 7.

WCAG 2.2 AA colour-contrast and screen-reader support throughout the website and chat widget.

A persistent, one-tap "Exit & clear history" control on every screen, and a visible "quick exit" button that instantly
redirects to an innocuous page (useful for users in unsafe environments).

Multilingual toggle (English, Twi, Ga, Ewe, Hausa at minimum) with content professionally translated, not
machine-translated only, for legal/safety material.

Mobile-first layout — most users will arrive via WhatsApp or a phone browser on modest data plans, so the
website should be lightweight and fast on 3G.


